                               MORNING



    She didn’t open it right away. She stood in the basement of the
Kingsbridge Armory and told the box something, quietly, in Kreyòl,
that I wasn’t meant to understand. Then she lifted the lid.
    We played them at the café on a Thursday.
    A restorer from the borough library had transferred them overnight.
Mr. Rodríguez had turned the back room into a theater with folding
chairs and two borrowed speakers, and it was full, rows deep, people
standing along the walls. Mama Tete sat in front with her hands folded
on her bag.
    Sofía pressed play.
    A crackle, a hiss, a hundred years of dust. Then a drum.
    It came out of the speaker like someone walking into a room he’d
been locked out of. Tall, unhurried, a hand on skin in 1925 on West
125th Street, and behind it a man singing under his breath in a voice
that cracked at the edges and didn’t care. I’d spent months listening to
the same eight seconds. This was a different eight. It went somewhere.
    Mama Tete didn’t move. Her face didn’t change. But the whole room
watched her shoulders come down one inch, the way Doña Luz’s had
when she said se calmó. She opened her hands on her bag and left them
open.
    When the first side ended, nobody clapped.
    Mr. Rodríguez walked to the butcher-paper map on the wall. He
took an index card out of his apron, wrote on it in his slow poet’s hand,
and pinned it beside a card that said Meridian Sound & Radio, W. 125th
St., 1924–1968.
    Papa Janvier. 1925. Six sides. RETENIDO → RELEASED.
