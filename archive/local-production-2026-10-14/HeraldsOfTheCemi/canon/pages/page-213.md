   So I did the only thing I had left.
   I knocked.
   I bent down and rapped my knuckles twice on the steel handle of
the trolley, flat and plain. The only knock I have.
   Two knocks.
   I’m here.
   It wasn’t loud. I don’t know if anyone else heard it. A trolley handle
in a hall full of horns. But Mikey’s head lifted a quarter of an inch, as if
somebody had touched him on the back of the neck.
   He looked at me.
   He unclasped his hands.
   He reached out, slowly, to the steel rail of the platform beside him,
and rapped it twice with his knuckles, flat and plain, across the width
of the Armory.
   I’m here.
   The hum stopped.
   I don’t mean it faded. I mean it cut off like a radio when someone
pulls the plug. One second the ring of forty was making a sound, and
the next there was a gap, a breath wide, where the sound had been.
   Mikey stood up.
   He was shaking all over. His face was wet. He put one hand on the
shoulder of the boy next to him, the twelve-year-old with the big dark
eyes, and said something quietly, and the boy looked up at him, and
Mikey lifted his hand in the air and held it there, palm out.
   Stop.
   Then he turned his head and looked at the door.
   Out.
   The boy stood up. A woman in the ring stood up. A man. Then two.
Then three. One by one, forty people got to their feet in the ring around
the platform like a field of grass after the wind has passed.
   Mikey walked down the three low steps to the carpet.
   His mother put out her arms.
   He walked into them like a man walking into water.
