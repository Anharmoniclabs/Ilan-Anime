   Manny saw me before I saw him. He stood up from a conga with
both arms out, a one-man welcome parade. “I had money on you bail‐

   “You sent me a voice memo of me being forced into consent.”
   “And it worked.” He steered me toward the ring. “Don’t think. Think‐
ing’s your whole problem. Just stand in it a second.”
   Then a voice from across the clearing, older, carrying without being
loud:
   “Let him stand where he can hear. He’s not ready to play. He thinks
he is, but he’s not.”
   The man who said it was well past sixty, with gray locs pulled back
and hands that looked like they’d been carved out of the same wood as
the drum between his knees. He wasn’t giving me the curiosity every
other person was giving the new guy. He was looking at me the way
the woman in the café doorway had. Not surprised. Like he’d been told
I was coming, and here I was, a little late, which tracked.
   And on a folding stool beside him, with a shekere in her lap and a
canvas grocery bag by her foot like she’d stopped on the way home
from the store, sat the woman from the café.
   She looked at me. This time she smiled, just at the corner, and said
something low to the man beside her. He nodded. I stood frozen at the
edge of the ring. Whatever had happened Friday night had been car‐
ried here, to this man, and he had been expecting me.
   “Rafa,” the old man said. “Manny’s been telling me about you for a
year. Yenni told me about you Friday.” He tilted his head toward the
woman, who lifted two fingers from the shekere. “My grandmother
called this an areíto,” he added, nodding at the ring. “The city calls it
unpermitted amplification. Sit, hijo. You’re not in trouble. You’re just
late.” The corner of his mouth moved. “Which I hear is on brand.”
   Manny snorted. I was going to kill Manny.
   I sat.
