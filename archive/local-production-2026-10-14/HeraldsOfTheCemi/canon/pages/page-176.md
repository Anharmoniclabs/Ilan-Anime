                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



    “Yanaisa told me.”
    “I’m telling you. Tercero.” She leaned toward the screen and looked
at me for a long moment, the way you look at a thing you’re packing
for a trip. “Come back.”
    Then she wanted to see the box. I swung the camera to La Madre’s
case. She had me lift the padding and show her underneath, and she
said, “Nada de plástico en su cara,” no plastic touching her face, and I
pulled out the last strip.
    Mama Tete had come up behind me holding a notebook of ad‐
dresses in several different hands and a bundle of letters bound with a
rubber band, envelopes stamped from places I knew only as shapes on
a map. She and Abuela had been calling the other houses, the names
on Abuela’s airmail letters, for weeks while I treated the Bronx as the
edge of the world.
    “It’s not just us,” said Mama Tete. “They did it in Kingston in Febru‐
ary. In Lagos in March. In Port-au-Prince they tried twice. We’re the
first night with a date. We’re not the only night.”
    She put her phone on the table, on speaker, and called five numbers
in a row.
    They answered from different hours of the night, in different ac‐
cents. Niamh Byrne, whom I’d already woken once, wanted to know
whether work boots were acceptable at three in the morning; her broth‐
er Fionn would be coming too, with a flask he declined to explain. Ruth
Adebayo, who kept the grounds near Stonehenge and mentioned it
apologetically, said she’d be up at three in the morning anyway, be‐
cause she started at four. Salma Hassan, a schoolteacher in Giza, in‐
formed us that her neighbor was bringing tea and that nobody should
ask about the pyramids. Lucía Huamán, who repaired woven cloth
near Chavín, said in slow, deliberate Spanish that she wouldn’t stage a
ceremony she didn’t keep just because a stranger in New York asked,
and that she would do what her family did and nothing more. Tomás
Aguilar, a mason near Teotihuacan, said, “I hit stones for a living. Is
that useful?”
