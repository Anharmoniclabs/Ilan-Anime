He took me to Sedgwick Avenue on Sunday night, after the circle,
when the sky was the color of an old bruise and the Cross Bronx
hummed in the distance like a refrigerator the size of a borough.
   “Everybody argues about it,” Rafa said. “Which room. Which night.
Who was there. I don’t care.” We stopped on the sidewalk in front of a
brick high-rise, gray, ordinary, with a blue sign on the corner post that
said HIP HOP BOULEVARD. “Put your hand on the wall.”
   It was just a building. Laundry hung in a window on the third floor.
Somebody’s television was on somewhere above. I put my palm flat
against the cold brick.
   The rec room, August 1973.
   A low-ceilinged space, concrete, linoleum, a folding table and a
hand-lettered banner: BACK TO SCHOOL JAM. It was ninety degrees
and the air smelled like sweat and hairspray and hot dogs from a grill
somebody had hauled in from the courtyard. Maybe seventy people.
Kids, mostly. A girl in charge of the cash box with the focus of a bank
manager.
   At the folding table, behind two turntables, stood a tall teenager
with his hands on a record.
   He didn’t say a word. He put one record on one turntable and an‐
other on the other, and I saw that they were the same record, two cop‐
ies, pressed from the same stamper. And I saw who was standing next
to him, a kid in shell-toe Adidas with a milk crate on his hip, setting
down the second copy. Todavía no, he’d said, in a record shop. Not yet.
Here he was, a block and a few days later, handing the second record to
the one who had the second turntable.
