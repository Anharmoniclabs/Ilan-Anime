                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



its foot lies the batey, rows of cinderblock houses in turquoise and pink,
a dirt yard, a colmado with a rusted Presidente sign, and in the yard,
twenty-two people in various states of costume trying to hold a re‐
hearsal.
    It was a gagá, the Easter band Wilfredo had told me about, and I
learned in about ninety seconds why people drive across the island for
one. A row of boys held bamboo trumpets, each with a single note, and
the tune only existed in the turns they took: one blew, the next
answered, the next, a melody nobody was carrying alone. Behind
them, tall drums, a woman in an enormous hat with a baton, and Wil‐
fredo’s uncle in a sequined dress, holding a beer.
    The leader was Wilfredo’s cousin Eliseo, who sat on a plastic chair in
a sequined cape and a Yankees cap with his head in his hands.
    Because it wasn’t working.
    It took me one pass to hear why. The horns came in half a beat be‐
hind the drums. Not badly. A hair. But a hair, in a thing like that, is a
canyon, and every time it opened, people on the drum side glared at
the horns, and people on the horn side glared back.
    “Ustedes llegan tarde,” a young drummer said.
    “Ustedes se adelantan,” an old woman answered. She was Haitian
by her accent and Dominican by her passport, and she held her trum‐
pet like a rifle. “Siempre se adelantan.”
    Each side was sure the other one was off.
    Abuela lowered herself onto a plastic chair under the chimney’s
shade. She folded her hands. She didn’t look at me. She looked at the
yard, and began to talk, quietly, in pieces, between the arguments.



“After the first dying,” she said, “the ones who ran went up into the
mountains. Taínos, first. Then, later, the others, the ones who got away
from the ships, Congos, Yorubas, from the same mills.” She watched
the horn players square off. “They didn’t speak each other’s language.
They had a drum on one side and a stone on the other. They put them
in the same ceremony and made a braid.”
