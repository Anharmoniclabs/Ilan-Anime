                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



Nine days to rent. The money I’d been counting on, put on hold by
people who were just being careful. I heard Manny saying it’ll cost you.
    I wasn’t supposed to be researching. I was supposed to be fixing the
mural I’d broken on purpose. What I was actually doing was looking
up spirals.
    It took four searches and one bad museum website. Then there it
was on the screen, in a glass case, photographed from three angles: a
three-pointed stone, dark, fist-sized, with a face carved into the front
and a spiral that opened into a triangle on the back. Zemi (cemí),
Dominican Republic, c. 1000–1500. The card said the Taíno thought a
cemí was both a spirit and the object that housed it, not a statue of the
thing but the thing. It said nobody knew what some of the carvings
meant.
    I took the coin out of my pocket and set it next to the screen.
    Same hooks. Same spiral. The same handwriting. My grandmother
had put it in my hand at twelve with no card, no museum, and no ex‐
planation, and a stone in a case in another country had been carved by
someone who wrote the same way.
    My grandmother keeps a bundle in the back of her closet in Santo
Domingo, wrapped in old cloth, about the size of two cupped hands. I
saw it once, on the only visit I remember, when I was eight. I reached
for it and she caught my wrist, gently, the way you catch a glass before
it falls. “No se toca a la Madre,” she said. You don’t touch the Mother.
Then she gave me a mango.
    I did not call my grandmother. Calling her would have made it a
thing.
    Instead I took out the contact mic puck, the one I’d been tuning at
the café, and set it on the oak table to run a quiet check on Chismosa.
The table was older than the building’s wiring. The radiator knocked.
The man snored. The reading lamp over my head buzzed on one thin
note. And for no reason I have ever been able to reconstruct, those
three lined up.
    The room filled.
