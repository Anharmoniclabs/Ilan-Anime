                         HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



   Folding tables where the bins would someday stand. One bare bulb.
A dozen women in kerchiefs and an old man or two, tying string
around bundles, with a hand-lettered sign taped to the wall: BOXES
FOR OUR BOYS — JEROME AVE. Nobody was in charge. Everybody
was doing everything. A box of socks hit the floor. Somebody cried
over it and somebody laughed at the crying.
   Then a woman stood on a chair.
   She wasn’t large. Hair under a scarf, sleeves to the elbow. She
clapped once, hard, and the room did what rooms do for a person who
has organized a thing before.
   “Tú atas,” she told a girl of fifteen. You tie. “Tú etiquetas.” You label.
“Tú cargas.” You carry. One job each, down the room, to a boy, to an
old man, to a woman with a baby on her hip. “Nadie levanta nada
solo.”
   Nobody lifts anything alone.
   The mess folded itself into a line. Boxes traveled hand to hand down
the table, tied, labeled, stacked. The baby laughed. The woman climbed
down and picked up the tape, and that was the whole speech. It lasted
maybe a minute and a half.
   Then the room was a café again, with six people looking at me.
   “Okay,” I said. “Who’s on water?”
   “Is that a toast?” said Manny.
   “It’s a plan.”



Here is what we built. For three weeks I’d been looking for a blade, a
trick, a page nobody had written yet. What we ended up with was a list
of chores, and every chore was about a door.
