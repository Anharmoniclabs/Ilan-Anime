                                  DINER



   “He calls the Bronx the pilot.” Mikey drank some coffee. “He’s got
speakers in Kingston, Lagos, Port-au-Prince. Tests. Nothing holds there
yet. He says the Bronx is where the count is thickest, so this is where it
holds first, and then the anchor travels.” He looked at me. “A thing like
that, sitting in the middle of a borough that just spent itself into the
floor? It doesn’t need speakers after. It just keeps pressing.”
   “Everywhere it goes.”
   “That’s the brochure,” said Mikey.



Gus came by with the pot and refilled our cups without being asked.
He looked at Mikey, and at me, and at the page between us, and then at
the cigarette burn on the table that had been there since before either of
us could read. He said, “You boys want pie?”
   “No, Gus.”
   “I’m putting it on the tab.”
   He went away. I put my hand flat on the tabletop to ground myself,
and the Formica was warm from the coffee, and under my palm
someone had carved two initials with a key.
   The diner turned over like a record.
   Christmas Eve, 1998. Same booths, same counter. Tinsel on the re‐
gister, a plastic wreath in the window, snow in the doorway where
somebody had propped the door. Gus, twenty-eight years younger, his
hair still black, in a paper hat, standing at the register with a thick
green ledger in his hands.
   The tab book.
   I’d seen it in real life, behind the counter, every day since I was ten.
A fat, stained accounting book where he wrote the names of everybody
on the block who ate on credit. Fiao, we called it. The Olympia ran on
fiao. It had run on fiao for sixty years.
   Gus opened it. The diner was full, dozens of people at the counter
and in booths, the sort of Christmas Eve crowd that comes because
nobody wants to be home or nobody has one. He began to read the
names off.
