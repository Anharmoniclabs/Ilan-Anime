    The airport was loud and I loved it. Spanish came at me faster and
looser than at home, full of words I half knew in rhythms I knew all the
way down. I did the thing I do in overwhelming places, the scatter, the
open tabs, and for once the scatter didn’t feel like a malfunction. It felt
like the correct tool. A guy tried to take my bag to “help,” and I clocked
the grift before he finished the sentence.
    My driver was a guy named Wilfredo, a religious medal swinging
off the mirror, who told me his whole life before we cleared the termin‐
al: his cousin in the Bronx, his mother’s people in Haina, his father’s
people across the border who didn’t discuss it.
    “How’s that going?” I said.
    “At Easter, everybody’s the same color of drunk.” He grinned in the
mirror. “My cousin leads a gagá in Haina. You come. Drums, güiras,
costumes, my uncle in a dress. Dominicans and Haitians. Nobody talks
about it. Everybody comes.”
    He turned the radio up. A bachata, a güira scraping along under it
like somebody sharpening a knife for supper. We hit the bridge over
the Ozama, and the tires sang on the steel joints, and Wilfredo’s fingers
drummed the wheel.
    The river went still.
    A wall of old stone above brown water. Seventeen years ago. A boy
of eight in a Yankees cap, with a mango too big for his hands, and be‐
side him a woman in a housedress with her hair tied in a scarf, holding
the boy’s other hand and looking at the river as if it owed her money.
    “Mira el río,” said my grandmother.
    “It’s dirty,” the boy said.
    “Es viejo.” Old. “Everything that’s been counted is dirty.”
    She took the boy’s free hand and turned it palm-up and drew a
shape in it with one finger. The spiral, then the triangle. The boy
giggled; it tickled. “Señales viejas,” she said. Old signs.
    Then she crouched, which cost her something, and put her face level
with his, and said it in Spanish, quietly, in the voice of someone who
knows a child will lose the words by morning.
