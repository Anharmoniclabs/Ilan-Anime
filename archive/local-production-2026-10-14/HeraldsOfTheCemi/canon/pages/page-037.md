   I stood on the sidewalk and listened to it on purpose.
   The merengue was fine. It was bright and bouncy. But under it, un‐
der the accordion and the güira and the horns, in the same place in
every bar, was a pressed-down place the width of a thumb. Eight
seconds. Then again. This time it was in the open air, and I was hearing
it with my own ears.
   One of the dominoes players slapped a tile too hard and said some‐
thing he’d have been embarrassed to say on a normal day. The other
guy said something back. The merengue kept going. A third guy
stepped out of the shop and told them both to lower their voices, in a
voice that wasn’t lower.
   Then, inside of a minute, it went out of all three of them. Nobody
apologized. They sat down the way you sit after carrying something
heavy up four flights and looked at the tiles, and the merengue kept
going.
   I had been hearing it for two months. I’d just been calling it the block.
