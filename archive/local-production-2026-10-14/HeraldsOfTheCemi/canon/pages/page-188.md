   “Yes.”
   “May I?”
   He asked the way you’d ask to hold a baby. I looked at his hand,
which had already begun to lift.
   I thought about a kitchen in 2007, and a radio, and a woman in a
dishtowel saying a word.
   “No, gracias,” I said.
   The hand stopped.
   For a moment nothing happened. Behind him, the champagne
glasses chimed. A waiter laughed.
   “Of course,” said Vale, pleasantly, and took out the black notebook,
and wrote a line.
   I didn’t see what.
   “I’m so glad to see you’ve brought your friend,” he said, turning,
and nodded at Manny, in his borrowed tuxedo. “Mr. Pérez, isn’t it?”
   Manny, who has the emotional range of a man who lies to every au‐
thority he’s ever met and has never once been caught, said, “Yes, sir.”
   “A lovely instrument.”
   “Thank you. It’s my aunt’s.”
   Vale looked at the conga, and at the cracked rim, and at Manny, with
great kindness. He wrote something else. Then he moved on, shaking
hands, and I let out a breath I’d been holding since the threshold.
   “My aunt’s?” said Manny, softly, to nobody. “I don’t even know why
I said that.”
