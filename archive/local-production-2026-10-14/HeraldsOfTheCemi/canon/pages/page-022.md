                         HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



   On the back wall, where the bins used to be, Mr. Rodríguez had
hung a map.
   It was hand-drawn, Jerome Avenue from 161st to 170th, in pencil
and marker on butcher paper, and it was covered in index cards. Each
card was a thing that used to be there, in somebody’s handwriting.
Casa Fela, shoe repair, 1968–1994. Don Chino’s barbershop (the line went out
the door on Sundays). The Rialto, 25¢ Saturdays. A new card went up
every week, from customers who’d stopped on their way out to remem‐
ber something. He’d been doing it for two years. When I asked him
why, he said, “So the block can check its own story.” When I asked him
again, he said something about poetry. I stopped asking.
   “Your building’s empty,” he said, tapping the paper, where the
corner of Jerome and our side street was blank. “The Ballroom. Sat‐
urday nights, mambo bands, my mother wore her best shoes. I have the
dates wrong, I know I do. You live upstairs. Ask your mother.” He
handed me a blank card and a marker. “Write it down when the rush
ends. We need the map.”
   “Why do we need it?”
   “Because when they change the block they change the story,” he
said, “and I’d like the story on paper first.”
   He said it like a weather report. Then the 4 went through and the
cups rattled and he was gone to a table, and I had a marker and a card
and no idea what I was supposed to know.



Manny showed up at 9:30, the minute the rush thinned enough that I
could pretend not to need him and he could pretend not to be helping.
   He comes through the door backward, shouldering it open with a
drum case in one hand and two iced coffees from the better place down
the block in the other, because Manny has opinions about our coffee
and drinks it free anyway. “For the labor,” he said, handing me one.
“I’m management now. I delegate hydration.”
