                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



    “There was a slide with a tree,” said Manny. “I cried at a Power‐
Point. A tree. It was a really good tree.” He shook his head. “I felt like
the best version of me, and that’s how I knew. The best version of me is
a little bit loud.”
    Vale had given a speech, forty minutes, soft, in his low even voice,
with eight-second pauses between the paragraphs. Sofía had counted.
He’d said neighbor forty-one times. He’d asked Manny’s last name, and
Manny had told him it was Pérez.
    “It’s my aunt’s,” said Manny. “I’m not smart. But I know when to
lie.”
    And Mikey. Mikey had stood at the back with his hands in front of
him like a man holding a coat. He’d never raised his head. When it was
over, he’d come to the drinks table and said his one sentence, and
Manny said Mikey’s hands were shaking when he said it, and that he’d
been humming under his breath the entire time. Eight seconds. A
pause. Again.
    “He looked at my hands,” said Manny, “like he was checking they
were still there.”



Rafa started the training on Wednesday, and he refused to call it that.
   He came into the café at ten, between the rush and the lull, ordered
a cortado, and sat in the corner under the poster for the open mic with
his coat on, looking at the room the way he’d looked at the clearing. He
waited until I’d wiped down the counter. Then he said, without raising
his voice, “Hold it.”
   “Hold what?”
   “The room.”
   There were maybe nine people in the café. A woman with a laptop.
Two kids sharing a muffin. The freelance saint in her corner. The
rumble of the El, the grinder, a radio at low volume with no pressed
place in it. I put my palm on the counter and found the pocket. I knew
what it felt like now: a place where all of it overlapped, slightly, like a
held breath.
