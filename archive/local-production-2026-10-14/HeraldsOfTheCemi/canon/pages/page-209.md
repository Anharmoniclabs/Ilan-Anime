             T R AC K   “Quimbara” — Celia Cruz and Johnny Pacheco
                        ECHO    the Armory brickwork, 1913




Mama Tete let the others see the room before she entered it.
    She stood in the loading doorway in her good dress and her
sunglasses, one hand on her cane, and watched the first thirty seconds
of what we had done to the room. Then she lifted two fingers, and the
boy in the red shirt lowered his horn, and the players behind him held
their breath.
    “Let them see us,” she said, to nobody. “Then we go.”
    They came in one at a time.
    A horn, a single note. A second horn answered it from a different
throat. A third waited until the first two had made room. That’s how a
gagá horn works: nobody carries the tune alone. The tune lives in the
turns they take, in the breath between the notes, in the way each player
listens for the next one. It took me about nine seconds to understand
what I was hearing, and then I stopped breathing.
    Nobody in it was on a click.
