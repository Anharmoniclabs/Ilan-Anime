   “Es un muchacho inquieto,” the man said. A restless kid.

   The woman folded the note along its crease and put it in the top
drawer of the dresser, the drawer I knew, the one that still sticks, the
one where my mother keeps her own papers now. She shut it with her
hip. Down the hall a radiator knocked, and the boy’s fingers found it,
and for a minute he was tapping in time with the building.
   I listened for the loop. Nothing pressed. Just a house at night, a kid
who didn’t fit a word, and two parents who didn’t know what else to
do with him.
   Nobody had told him either. That was all I got from it, and that
night it was enough.
   The light went gray. The ceiling was only my ceiling. The 4 went by.
   My phone buzzed.

I didn’t answer. I was busy.
   The clinic was the third one I tried that had an opening, a sliding-
scale place on the second floor of a building on Webster, over a check-
cashing storefront. Dr. Okafor, adult evaluations. First appointment:
Thursday, 8:15 a.m. I booked it before my brain could finish an objec‐
tion. It asked me to bring a list of concerns.
   The form had a box at the bottom. Reason for visit (optional).
   I typed I think my brain is a radio. I deleted it.
   I typed attention. I deleted that.
   I typed the one thing I was sure of, and hit submit.
   Tapping.
