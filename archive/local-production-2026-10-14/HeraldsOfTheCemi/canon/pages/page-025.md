    The steamer’s whistle, which had been sitting on a clean A, sagged
half a step flat. Just for a second. Manny’s palm was still on his drum
case, and it came back.
    My thumb was over the screen. It would have been so easy. Open it.
Read the rest. See the number.
    I forwarded the preview to Sofía with three words, is this anything,
and put the phone face down. I did it before my brain finished arguing,
and my brain, a second late, delivered the reason: a cold pull low in my
stomach, the feeling of a hand on my sleeve at the curb. No. Not them.
    “What was that face,” Manny said.
    “Spam.”
    He held out his hand. I’ve never been able to lie to Manny. I turned
the phone over and let him read it.
    “Bro.” His eyebrows went up for real. “Hardware? A budget? A
commission at the Armory? That’s a real stage, JC. Tell me the move is
you call them back.”
    “I’m not calling them back.”
    “Why.” And he wasn’t being a jerk about it, which is what made it
hard. He was being my friend. He’d watched me skip lunch for two
weeks and do the rent math out loud without hearing myself. “You’re
killing yourself on three gigs. Your moms worries. These people want
to pay you for the exact thing you made. I’m not saying marry them.
I’m saying let them buy you a nice laptop.”
    I had nothing to put on the other side of the scale. Everything he
said was true and kind. All I had was a cold feeling in my gut and the
certainty of a man who’d been awake for twenty-eight hours.
    “I can’t explain it,” I said. “I’m not doing it.”
    Manny looked at me for a long second. Then he picked up his coffee.
    “Okay,” he said. “Last time you couldn’t explain it, it was the dude
selling Hennessy out a Honda, and you were right.” He drank. “It’ll
cost you, though. Real money.”
    “I know.”
