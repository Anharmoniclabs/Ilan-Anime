                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



He looked at that page the way you’d look at a photo of your own
house from above.
   “What happens at the Armory?” I said.
   “They call it a baseline.” He pushed the page back toward me.
“Everybody gets a little calmer. It’s eleven minutes. There’s a platform
in the middle with a ring of seats around it. People like me sit in the
ring.”
   “Why?”
   “Because we hold it.” He wiped his mouth. “Everybody who got
through the program, the ones who still hear it. We’re the tuning forks.
If enough of us hold the tone, people around us settle. Even if the
speakers stop. It spreads.” He hesitated. “The instruction sheet says to
lead them through. Show the room a settled body. Keep the tone going
through any disturbance.”
   He pulled a folded paper from inside his jacket and held it for a
second without opening it.
   “And there’s a line,” he said. “At the bottom. I don’t know what it
means.”
   He passed it to me.
   The visiting artist’s family object may serve as the anchor.
   I read it twice.
   The coin sat warm against my thigh. I thought about Vale on a land‐
ing, saying bring what you like, and about a stone in a green safe that
wasn’t green anymore after you’d stared at it long enough.
   “The family object,” I said.
   “They told me you’d bring one.” His jaw tightened. “They’re count‐
ing on it. A thing that answers loud. If she answers them, if she sits up
on that platform and takes their note, the whole borough locks on it.
Like a switch.”
   “Did they say that?”
   “They said ‘anchor.’ They said it travels. I was in the room.” He
rubbed his eyebrow, the one with the scar. “I’m not as dumb as I act.”
   “You don’t act dumb.”
   “I act calm,” said Mikey. “Same thing.”
   “What happens after?” I said. “If it works.”
