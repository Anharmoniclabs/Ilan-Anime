    The kid grinned. “Todavía no.”
    He paid in quarters. He left with the crate. The man behind the
counter shook his head at the door like the boy had told him a good lie.
    And at the far end of the counter, where the sunlight didn’t reach,
stood a third man.
    A thin man in a brown suit, a small notebook. He hadn’t bought
anything. He was writing, and he wrote quick and neat, a mark for
each. His eyes followed the kid to the door, and then followed the door,
and then the street, as if the street were also part of the sale. When the
kid was gone, he ticked something. And then he looked up, not at the
man behind the register, and not at the room, but at the exact spot in
the air where I was standing, in a café fifty-three years from where he
was.
    I’ve seen that way of holding a head exactly once. It was in a lit win‐
dow at three in the morning, on a man in headphones.
    “—JC. JC.”
    Manny’s hand was on my shoulder. The latte was overflowing, milk
running down the pitcher and over my knuckles, hot, and I hadn’t felt
it. The freelancer was staring. Mr. Rodríguez was halfway across the
room already, moving the way he moves when something’s about to hit
the floor.
    “I’m good.” My voice came from the other side of a wall. “I lost the
pour.”
    “You went somewhere.” Low, just for me. The stool, the drum case,
his palm still flat on it, and the room, all of it, slid back into its track.
“That wasn’t a tired thing.”
    “It was a tired thing.”
    “JC.”
    “It was a tired thing, Manny.” I wiped the milk off my hand. The
skin was pink and fine. I dumped the ruined latte and started another,
steady, performing steady. “I didn’t eat. I told my mom I’d eat. I didn’t
eat.”
