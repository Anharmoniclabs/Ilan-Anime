    The floor went.
    The drill hall, 1926. The white carpet was gone. In its place, a hun‐
dred and fifty feet of wooden planking, polished to a sheen, running
wall to wall. And the room was in two states at once.
    At the far end, two hundred men in uniform drilled in lines. Rifles at
port arms. A sergeant’s voice calling cadence. Four hundred boots
striking the planks at the same instant, a single enormous crack, then
another, a sound with no gap in it, no air. The planking took it like a
drumhead. A sound of obedience. A sound that only works if nobody’s
listening to anything else.
    And at the near end, behind a rope, another two hundred people
were dancing.
    It was a Saturday night in the Jazz Age and the armory had been
rented out for a ball. A swing band on a riser. Women in dresses with
fringe. Men with their ties loosened. The same wood floor, the same
steel roof overhead, taking the sound of four hundred feet moving out
of step, in dozens of different rhythms at once, bouncing off the walls
in great loose sprays of laughter and brass.
    They were using the same floor.
