                               MORNING




At the center of the drill hall Mr. Rodríguez had set a table, covered it
with a white cloth, and put nothing on it for twelve hours, because he
said it would know when it was time.
   La Madre had spent the year in Mr. Rodríguez’s green safe. At sev‐
en, my grandmother took her out and laid her on the cloth.
   At 7:05, Yanaisa unwrapped her sling and set Yúcahu beside her. A
smaller stone, darker, round-shouldered, its face turned a little away.
Mother and son, on a tablecloth in the Kingsbridge Armory, like two
people at a wedding who’d been seated separately and had finally
found each other.
   Abuela looked at me.
   “Ahora sí,” she said.
   I took the coin out of my pocket. I’d carried it fourteen years. It had
never been mine. It had only been on loan. It was warm, but a warmth I
could stand.
   I set it in the broken point of La Madre’s crown, where the cord and
the resin held the place open, and it fit. It settled, the way a word
settles back into the sentence it was cut from.
   When I put my palm on it the hum came back as one steady note,
with no seam in the middle where the two halves used to meet.
   The hall made no sound. Then, from somewhere in the crowd, a
very small boy said, “Is that it?” and a hundred people laughed, and
Abuela, who hadn’t caught the English, laughed with them.



At 9:40, Sofía rang a kitchen timer into a microphone.
   It was an egg timer, shaped like a chicken. It went off with a sound
like a tiny, furious bicycle.
   “This,” said Sofía, “is the Moment of Noise.”
   The Armory exploded.
   Four thousand people in a drill hall the size of an airport, every one
of them making sound on purpose, as hard as they could, for eleven
minutes. Pots. Pans. Congas, bucket drums, bamboo horns. Rafa’s palo.
