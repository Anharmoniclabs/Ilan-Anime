                           FOUNDERS’ NIGHT



    She walked us through it in six minutes. The exits were marked and,
on paper, clear. The loading passage, where the band would come in,
was behind a heavy curtain at the north end of the hall. The green
room had a door and an attendant. The center platform seated forty.
The audience seating, according to the board’s conditions, was open
and unreserved.
    “The internal sheet has four movements,” she said. “Gathering,
counting, calm, seating. I asked what seating meant. They told me it
was a technical term.” She looked at the case on the trolley. “There’s a
pedestal in the middle of the platform. Nobody’s told me what goes on
it.”
    “It isn’t full,” she said, and I saw her almost smile. “They wanted
five thousand registered. They got eleven hundred. The rest is the
block.”
    I looked toward the entrance.
    The block was arriving.
    Doña Fela in a hat. Papo and Wilson, side by side, in sport coats they
had borrowed from the same cousin. Don Chino, in a tie, with his wife
on his arm, looking at the champagne tower with the expression of a
man being asked to trust a frozen chicken. Yenni’s daycare mothers. A
bunch of the Soundview kid’s friends. Teodoro from the café, who’d
heard there would be a program and had brought a poem. People who’d
seen the flyer and thought free food, people who’d heard the hearing on
the radio and thought I’d like to see this, and in the middle of them a wo‐
man I’d never met, in a church coat, who had brought her own folding
chair because she’d been to these things before.
    “Open admission,” said Sofía, behind me, with a satisfaction she’d
never admit. “They had to take whoever came.”
    She was in a black dress with a handbag that had, I knew, four
phones in it.
