                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



   A bank, 1962. The lobby had a high painted ceiling and a row of
brass-barred teller windows, and somewhere behind the tellers a radio
was playing “Mack the Knife” in the cheerful key of a man being told
about a murder. At a desk behind a low rail sat a young couple, her
gloves folded in her lap, his suit one size too big, and across from them
a loan officer with a manila folder and a smile with no teeth in it. He
slid a paper back across the desk. She read it. She read it again. His
hand found hers.
   At the next desk over, a thin man in a brown suit was writing in a
small notebook.
   He didn’t look at the couple. He wrote the address they’d asked
about. When they stood and left, he watched them go to the door and
ticked something, and the radio played on.
   The echo skipped, the way a record skips. Same desk. A month later.
The thin man had a deed folder open in front of him, and the address
on it was the one the couple had wanted, at a price that made the clerk
whistle.
   He hadn’t drawn the line. Somebody else had drawn it, somewhere
in a government office, with a ruler and a rubber stamp. The man in
the brown suit had just read it, and known exactly what it was worth.
   The lobby fell away. The waiting room came back, the TV muted, the
fish glaring at me with a beady eye. I realized I was tapping the glass.
   “Mr. Cabral?” A woman in the doorway, tablet in hand. “I’m Dr.
Okafor.”



She was younger than I’d expected and faster, and within about four
minutes I understood that she did this all day, that I wasn’t a special
case, and that the things I was about to say in a voice cracking with
how much they cost were things she’d heard a thousand times over
that fish. She asked about school, work, sleep, the trains. She asked
about noise. When I told her about the café, how I could think in a loud
room and not in a quiet one, how I started everything on the 4, she
didn’t look up from her tablet.
