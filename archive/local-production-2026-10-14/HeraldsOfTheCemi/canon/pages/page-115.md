                         C H A P T E R T W E LV E


                  Learning to Hold
                   T R AC K    “Apache” — Incredible Bongo Band
                   ECHO       1520 Sedgwick Avenue, August 1973




Yari met me at arrivals in scrubs, holding two breakfast sandwiches
like a hostage negotiator.
    “You smell like another country,” she said, and hugged me for
slightly longer than a friend does, and slightly less than anyone else
would.
    It was seven on Friday morning. She’d come straight from a twelve-
hour shift and had the face of a woman who had recently told a man
not to swallow batteries. I handed her my carry-on and kept the back‐
pack, and she noticed, because she notices everything, and lifted it by
the strap.
    “It’s warm.”
    “It’s a heating pad.”
    “Juan Carlos.”
    I looked at the arrivals board for a while. “It’s my grandmother’s.
It’s a family thing. I’m not allowed to say yet.”
    Yari studied me with the particular patience of an ER nurse who has
heard many versions of it’s nothing. Then she let the strap go. “Okay.”
    “Okay?”
    “I’m not asking. I’m telling you what I did, because I did it.” She bit
into a sandwich. “You missed Okafor on Thursday. I called. You’re res‐
cheduled for Monday, 8:15. You’re going.”
