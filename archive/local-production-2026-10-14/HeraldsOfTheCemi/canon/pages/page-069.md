   I took the flyer out of my pocket to show him, the one I’d gotten at
the park, and smoothed it flat on the counter. And in the bottom corner,
in the little gray type you only read when you’re looking for it:
   Founders’ Night: A Celebration of Our Community. June 13. Kingsbridge
Armory.
   Rafa leaned over my shoulder and read it. He didn’t say anything.
His mouth got very small.
   “Three months,” I said.
   “Mm.” He folded the flyer once, along its crease, and handed it back
like it was a note from a dead man. “A listening session is a rehearsal,
hijo. That’s opening night.”
   Then he held out his palm. “Show me the thing in your pocket.”
   I laid the coin on his hand. He didn’t pick it up. He bent to look, the
way you look at a baby that isn’t yours, and his face did something
quick and complicated and was gone.
   “Señales viejas,” I said. “That’s what my grandmother calls them.”
   “She’s right.” He straightened and I put it back in my pocket. “Don’t
show that to the man with the flyers. Men like that like to know what
you’re carrying.”



The clinic was on the second floor of a brick building on Webster, over
a check-cashing place, and the waiting room had six chairs, a TV bolted
to the wall playing a court show with the sound off, and a fish tank.
One fish. A sign on the glass said PLEASE DO NOT TAP THE TANK,
and I looked at it for a long time, because it was the first sign in my life
that had ever described me.
   I was early, which has happened to me twice. I sat with my back
against the radiator, and the radiator knocked, and the tank’s bubbler
hissed, and from the check-cashing place below, through the floor,
came the faint hum of a TV.
   Marble came up under my feet.
