                                   DINER



    Mikey looked at the table.
    “I know,” he said. “The light was on.”
    “I didn’t know you were outside.”
    “You knew somebody was.”
    “Yes.”
    He was quiet for a while. A truck idled outside. The jukebox clicked
through a song nobody had paid for.
    “I’m not mad about the text,” said Mikey. “I mean. I was. I was real
mad.” He turned his cup a quarter turn in its saucer. “But the text
wasn’t the thing. It was the two years.”
    “I know.”
    “Two years you walk past my mother’s door like it’s a hole in the
floor.”
    “Yeah.”
    “She leaves you food.”
    “Mamá leaves her food.”
    “Same difference.” He looked up. His eyes were very tired and very
clear. “Don’t say sorry again. It’s a word. I’ve heard a lot of those. They
sit there.”
    “What do you want?”
    “Knock,” said Mikey.
    “On what?”
    “On anything.” He almost laughed. “We had a language, remember?
Two knocks. I’m here. You walk past my door for two years, you don’t
know what you missed. You don’t say sorry. You knock.”
    I let it sit. I pictured a boy at nine on a landing with his ear against a
wall, saying the building was humming.
    “Okay,” I said.
    “Okay.”
    He didn’t put out two fingers. He didn’t ask me to. He put his hand
flat on the table next to mine, not touching, a few inches away, and left
it there like a man setting something down on a counter to be picked
up later.
