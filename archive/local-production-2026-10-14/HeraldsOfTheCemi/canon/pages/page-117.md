                          LEARNING TO HOLD




The basement of El Disco Café still had the stock from the record shop.
   Not all of it. Mr. Rodríguez had sold what he could and thrown out
what mold had claimed, but along the back wall, in wooden crates
stacked to the ceiling, were records in paper sleeves from 1989, because
nobody had the heart to sell them and nobody had the heart to throw
them out. It smelled of cardboard and damp stone. Overhead, the 4
went through the foundations like a long slow thought.
   In the corner, set into the wall, was a safe. Green, steel, a Mosler,
with a brass dial.
   “My father kept the money in it,” said Mr. Rodríguez. “It’s been
empty since the year he died.” He didn’t ask what was in the bundle.
I’d wrapped it in a pillowcase, because I’m an idiot, and then in a
second pillowcase. He looked at the cloth for a long moment. “My
mother had something wrapped like that. In a closet. I never asked.”
He turned the dial, and the door swung. “I should have asked her.
Once.”
   I set La Madre on the empty shelf. The coin, in my pocket, went hot,
and then, as the door closed, settled. Down there, with the El going
through the stones, nothing was pressed. I stood in a basement in the
Bronx and, for the first time since January, heard nothing under the
sound.
   He gave me the key. “You keep it,” he said. “I’m old. If I have it, I’ll
lose it.”



Manny told me about the session on Saturday, over pastelitos, with
Sofía across the table taking notes in a notebook labeled DATA.
   “It was beautiful,” he said. “That was the problem.”
   Sofía read from the notebook. “Ninety minutes. Never above sixty-
two decibels. Zero raised voices. Forty-one attendees, thirty-nine of
whom told me afterward, unprompted, that they felt ‘really good.’”
She looked up. “I hate that I felt really good.”
