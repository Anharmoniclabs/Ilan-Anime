                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



    “First Sunday of the month he comes. Groceries. An envelope. He
sits where you’re sitting. He calls me señora.” Her voice finally did
something. It wavered, once, like a candle in a draft. “Two months ago
he was washing a plate. He hummed. Eight seconds, again and again,
no song in it. Just that. I asked him what it was. He said, ‘What is
what?’”
    I didn’t move. I couldn’t have.
    “I know what you’re going to ask,” she said. “Because the child who
knocked on the wall can hear things. Mikey could too. He said the
building hummed. And you’re going to ask what that was, and I don’t
know. But I know it was in him that day, in my kitchen, and it wasn’t
his.”
    The window rattled. A 4 train went by. Behind me, in the fridge, the
compressor kicked on, and for one second the whole kitchen was loud
and alive.
    “He came home that night,” she said. “The night in February. One in
the morning. He said, ‘JC’s up. His light is on.’ He went out. He came
back in an hour and said nothing. Two weeks later he didn’t sleep here
anymore.”
    She watched me and didn’t fill the silence.
    “You were his brother,” said Doña Luz. “You went to your music,
and he went to whoever answered the phone.”
    “Yes, señora.”
    I didn’t say anything else. She let me sit in it. Then she stood up and
put her hand on the Pyrex like it was a Bible.
    “Don’t go to their meetings,” she said. “They’ll take you too. And
bring me my son.”
    Both of those. In one breath. I’ve never been asked to do two oppos‐
ite things so clearly.



I sat on the landing step outside her door with my back against the
wall and my palm on the iron banister, and I couldn’t make myself
leave.
