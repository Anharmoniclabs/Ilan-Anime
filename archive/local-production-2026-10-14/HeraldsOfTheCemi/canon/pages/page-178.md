                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



   She paid with exact change. On the way home she carried the bag in
one hand and held my arm with the other, which she hadn’t done since
I was nine, and I didn’t say anything at all.



Yanaisa arrived on Friday, with Yúcahu in a Tupperware.
    That was Abuela’s idea. She had packed the small stone in dish tow‐
els under a layer of sancocho in a gray plastic container and told my
cousin that no customs officer in the world would dig through a grand‐
mother’s soup. At JFK they made her open it. The officer, a large, tired
man with a tattoo of a rose on his wrist, looked at the soup, and at
Yanaisa, and said, in the voice of a man who had been somebody’s
grandson too, “Go. Go on.”
    “I didn’t even have to say anything,” said Yanaisa, setting the Tup‐
perware on our kitchen table. “He cried a little.”
    She unwrapped the towels slowly, and a dark round shoulder of
stone came into the light.
    The coin in my pocket pulled toward it, a small, firm weight, and my
palm was on the edge of the table before I decided anything.
    “Don’t,” said Yanaisa.
    “I wasn’t.”
    “You were thinking about it.”
    Mamá put a plate of rice between us, which ended the demonstra‐
tion, and turned to my cousin with the tender, terrifying courtesy she
reserves for relatives. “You’ll sleep in my room. Eat first.”
    Yanaisa ate. She ate the way people eat when someone has been
waiting for them. Over her shoulder I could see my mother watching
her fork.
    After dinner Manny and I carried the cases down to the landing, be‐
cause he wanted to rehearse.
    We sat on the stairs. He tapped the rim of his cracked conga twice. I
tapped my knee twice. He waited thirty seconds, which turned out to
be a long time to wait with a man who wants to talk, and tapped again.
I tapped back.
