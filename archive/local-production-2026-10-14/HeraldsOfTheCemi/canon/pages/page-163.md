He didn’t know about the buildings.
    I figured that out about thirty seconds after I slid the page out of my
bag and set it down beside the sugar. The observation report, four ad‐
dresses, the date on the bottom. I’d meant to wait. I’d meant to let him
tell me his part first. But I’m not made for waiting at a table with some‐
thing like that in my pocket.
    Mikey read it. His eyes went down the list, the café, the Ballroom, a
number I didn’t know, and stopped.
    “That’s my mother’s building.”
    “That’s our building.”
    “Eighty-one tenants.” He read the line again, his lips moving. “Un‐
safe occupancy. Closure recommended.” His voice dropped. “June
fourteenth. They don’t say how.”
    “They don’t have to. It’s written ahead.”
    He stared at the page.
    “They never said that,” said Mikey. “They said there’s an allowance.
They said as long as I’m in good standing, my mother stays in the pro‐
gram. They said she gets the support.” His thumb found the seam of
the booth’s vinyl seat and began working at it. “They said nothing
about the building.”
    “I know.”
    “I thought I was keeping her in it.”
    He didn’t hum. I want to say that. I’d been braced for it, braced for
the low note through closed lips and the eight-second gap. It didn’t
come. Whatever was happening in Mikey’s chest was too loud for it.
