                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



   I looked at my friend, bent over his cracked drum in the aisle with a
hand pressed to his ribs, grinning through his teeth like an idiot, and I
looked at the stone. And I thought, they’ve written a page on every
move I might make but one. Nobody writes a page for a man who just
stands there with his hands open.
   I turned my palms up.
   I held them up to the ceiling, to Vale’s voice, in front of eleven hun‐
dred people and a supervisor.
   “No, gracias,” I said.



The old man in the yellow vest came down the east aisle at a speed I’d
have said was impossible for a person of seventy.
    “Excuse me!” said Mr. Rodríguez. “Excuse me!”
    The vest said COMMUNITY OBSERVER. He held the radio in one
hand and a clipboard in the other and wore the expression of a man
who had waited his entire life to say what he was about to say.
    “As the community observer designated by Community Board Sev‐
en,” he announced, to Duane, to the staff, to the entire drill hall, in his
big poet’s voice, “I am noting on the record, at nine forty-three p.m.,
that the north exit is obstructed.”
    He pointed at the curtain across the loading passage.
    “Condition two of the permit, as read into the minutes on May
twenty-eighth: exits to be unlocked and unobstructed throughout.” He
clicked his pen. “I am also noting, at nine forty-three, that a participant
has been physically restrained. Please state your name for the record.”
    “Sir,” said Duane, “this is a private event.”
    “It’s a permitted event.” He wrote something. “Your name.”
    The attendants, on either side, had gone very still. These were em‐
ployees. They’d been told customer service, and a seventy-year-old man
with a clipboard was saying record, and name, and permit, and every one
of them had a mother, and every mother knows what a man with a
clipboard can do.
