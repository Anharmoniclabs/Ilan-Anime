    Vale arrived at seven, alone, with a slim portfolio, and shook hands
with the chair.
    He presented for eleven minutes. He spoke without notes and with
the low, warm pulse of a man who’d said these things at many tables.
A celebration of community. An investment in wellness. A night of
calm. Safety plans, certified. He paused after each paragraph. I coun‐
ted, I couldn’t help it. By the third pause, I saw two board members
rest their hands flat on the table, as if to steady it. By the fifth, the wo‐
man in front of me had stopped fanning herself.
    I wanted to stand up and put a radio on the floor.
    “Public comment,” said Mr. Salcedo. “Two minutes. Name and
block.”
    I’d signed up eleventh.
    By the ninth speaker, I was holding my paper in a grip I had to
loosen finger by finger. By the tenth, a woman in a church dress was at
the microphone saying her granddaughter’s school had a quiet room
and was it supposed to be that quiet, and Mr. Salcedo thanked her and
told her the board would take it under advisement.
    My name.
    I stood. I walked to the microphone. The floor was linoleum, buffed,
the kind that squeaks. I gripped the metal stand.
    The same auditorium, a long time ago. 1975. Folding chairs, a ban‐
ner that said BRONX COMMUNITY BOARD, a thick air of cigarette
smoke and stale coffee. A woman in a church dress stood at the same
microphone with a baby on her hip. She was young, and she was shak‐
ing.
    “Ustedes no pueden cerrar la estación,” she said. You can’t close the
firehouse.
    “Señora,” said the chair, a man with a mustache and a tired kind
face, “your two minutes began. Do you have a statement?”
    “My baby couldn’t breathe,” she said. “The truck came in four
minutes. If you close it, it’s fourteen. That’s all I have. I don’t have a pa‐
per.”
