                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



Manny’s conga, repaired by a woman in Hunts Point whose bill we’d
paid in full. Desmond holding a bus horn he’d liberated from a depot.
Kids with whistles. Phones, every phone, each on its own ringtone, so
that a thousand different songs went off at once and became, on the far
side of enough of them, a kind of weather.
   On the big screens above the stage, five squares: a riverbank in Ire‐
land, a fencepost on a rainy hill, a roof in Giza, a courtyard with a
lamp, and a wall that had no business being so beautiful.
   And at the edge of the last square, in a mill yard under a stopped
chimney, Eliseo in his sequined cape, leading twenty-two people in a
gagá, Haitian and Dominican, the young drummer beside the old wo‐
man with the gray braids, his uncle in a dress, holding a beer, and the
whole thing locked.
   I sat in a chair at the side, in the one spot Yari said I could, with my
left ear to the room, and did nothing at all.
   It was the hardest thing I have ever done.
   I went looking for the flat spot anyway. I couldn’t help it. I listened
for the pressed place under the sound in every corner of that enormous
hall, under every drum and horn and ringtone.
   There wasn’t one.
   Manny came and sat beside me at 9:48. He didn’t speak. He put his
big hand flat on the case of his drum, across his knees, and I felt the
room settle into a groove I knew.
   At 9:51 Sofía rang the chicken.
   Afterward there was a long silence, and in it four thousand people
breathing out.
   “Next year,” said Mama Tete, beside me. “And the year after. They’ll
ring somewhere else. We answer again.”
