                              THE MODEL



   “Your turn,” Sofía said. “What were you looking at when I walked
in?”
   So I told her the part I could say out loud.
   I told her about the loop.
   I’d saved it from the raw feed that night, the eight seconds pulled
out of everything else and looped on its own. I put one earbud in her
hand. She listened with the face she uses on contracts, and then she
took it out.
   “That’s a loop,” she said. “It sounds like every hold music in Amer‐
ica.”
   “It’s under everything on the block. Car stereos. The bodega. A kid’s
phone on the stoop. It doesn’t have a melody, it just makes everything
feel…” I looked for the word. “Pressed flat. And the block’s been mean
lately. You’ve noticed it.”
   “I’ve noticed it’s been a long winter and the landlord on Creston is a
monster.” She set the earbud down carefully, as if it were evidence. “JC.
I believe you heard something. I believe you didn’t hallucinate it, be‐
cause you’re the least delusional creative I’ve ever met, which is a low
bar but still. What I don’t have is a number. You want to tell me a sound
is making the block mean. What’s it doing, to how many people, com‐
pared to what?” She raised a finger before I could answer. “And before
you say it: yes, I know how that sounds like I’m calling you crazy. I’m
not. I’m saying I need a control. A block without it.”
   “A block without it.”
   “Same month, same weather, same rent, no loop. Then we com‐
pare.” She pulled up another tab. This one was labeled SOUND OF
THE BLOCK in all caps. “And speaking of the loop. The music-licens‐
ing arm is giving away free in-store music systems. January. The bo‐
dega, the laundromat, the barbershop, two nail salons, the dentist on
the corner. Free speakers, free playlists, free ‘ambiance.’ You’ve heard
them.”
   I’d heard them. I’d heard them every day for two months without
hearing them at all. That’s the trick of ambient: it’s music for people
who don’t want to listen.
