    “Two incidents at the café in two and a half hours. One of them was
the parking ticket. Nineteen at the bodega. Junior says somebody
threw a domino at a pigeon. And after every one it’s the same, he says.
Five minutes of yelling, then everybody sits there like somebody pulled
their plug.” She turned the clipboard around. “And then there’s this.”
    She’d drawn a line through the middle of the sheet. Above it, the
tally. Below it, a single word in pen.
    Zero.
    “Twenty-six minutes,” she said. “Not one raised voice in a room
with forty people, two babies, and a man in the back who’d been drink‐
ing since six. Zero.” She exhaled. “A control group is not supposed to
feel like this, JC. A control group is supposed to be boring.”
    “I can’t explain it.”
    “I’m not asking you to. I’m asking you to let me sit on a curb for four
minutes and not be a scientist.” She put her head on her knees. “I need
a floor.”
    Manny found us there, carrying a tray of pastelitos he’d earned and
the glow of a man who’s been told he was good by strangers. He
lowered himself onto the curb like he was setting down a piano.
    “Yo.” He was out of breath. “Did the wall do that because of me, or
did you press a button?”
    “Both.”
    “That was sick.” He chewed. “Wait. Was it reading the drum, or was
it reading my mind? Because if it’s reading my mind I want to see what
it said. I’d like to be told I’m smart.”
    “It’s reading the drum.”
    “Ah.” He seemed to take that as a disappointment he could live
with. He chewed another pastelito in silence, and I watched him try to
think something and not find the door. “It was weird, though,” he said
finally. “I don’t know. Like I wasn’t playing the drum, I was playing the
room. Like the room was a conga and somebody had finally tuned it. Is
that dumb?”
    “No.”
    “Okay, it’s a little dumb.” He stuck a pastelito in my hand. “Sunday.
Rafa’s circle. In the park. You come. You don’t even have to play.”
