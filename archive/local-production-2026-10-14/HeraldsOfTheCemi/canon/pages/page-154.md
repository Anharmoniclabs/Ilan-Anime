                         HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



   “Anchor,” said Manny. “Anchored to what?”
   Nobody at the table knew yet.



There was a second page.
   Vanessa slid it across to me without a word. It had my name at the
top. Not my whole name. Subject: J. C. Cabral. Below it, in a plain gray
font, a list of categories. Money pressure. Hunger for praise. Duty to family.
Loyalty to friends. And under each, in sentences, not numbers, a predic‐
tion.
   Refuses the first offer, to keep his story about himself intact.
   Returns when the people around him begin paying for his refusal.
   Will accept a favor if it arrives dressed as an honor.
   “That,” said Manny, reading over my shoulder, “is you. You’d take a
compliment from a parking meter.”
   I wanted to be angry. What I felt was something worse: recognition,
the way you feel when somebody reads your handwriting aloud. They
had known I’d refuse. They’d known the refusal would hold for a
month. They’d known where the money would run out, who would
call, what praise would do. They’d given my anger a chair, and my
stubbornness a chair, and what I thought of as my conscience a small
side table.
   “They told me this was a kindness,” said Vanessa. “A way to be
gentle with you.”
   “Is it?”
   “I couldn’t tell. So I brought it.” Her mouth moved. “I don’t think I
thought it through until I was in the car.”
   Danny, in the corner, cleared his throat. “Can I say something?”
   Everyone looked at him. He had the ice-pack face of a kid who’d
been trying to say something for ten minutes.
   “I think you two know each other,” he said.
   Vanessa turned. A beat. Then she said, flatly, in disbelief:
   “You’re the night listener.”
   “You’re the chairs lady.”
