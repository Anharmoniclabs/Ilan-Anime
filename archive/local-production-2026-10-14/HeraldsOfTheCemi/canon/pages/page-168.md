                         HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ




We stayed another hour. We didn’t solve anything. He told me, in bits,
in the neutral voice he used when he was ashamed, about the boxes
he’d installed in the shops, and the ones he’d gone back to when
people unplugged them, and the discount he’d said depended on the
service. He told me which ones. He said he’d tell Sofía, but not yet. I
said okay. He said I said not yet. I said I heard you. He watched my face
until he believed it.
    He took the tenants’ lawyer’s number out of his jacket pocket,
Dalia’s, where I’d slipped it when we hugged, and looked at it, and put
it back.
    “Don’t use my mom,” he said. “To make me do something.”
    “I won’t.”
    “And don’t tell everybody I’m coming.”
    “I won’t.”
    Gus brought the check. Mikey paid while I was still reaching for my
wallet. I left the tip, which felt like a transaction too small to count and
exactly the right size.
    On the sidewalk, in the light, in front of a window painted with pan‐
cakes nobody has ever actually been served, Mikey stood with his
hands in his pockets.
    “You’re going to be in the middle,” he said.
    “What?”
    “The seating. I saw the chart before I came. Your seat’s in the center,
behind the ring.” His voice dropped. “They don’t want you near the
doors.”
    “I figured.”
    “And when you walk in,” said Mikey, “don’t let them put your
grandmother’s thing in the green room.”
    “Why?”
    “It’s the quietest room in the building.” His eyes held mine. “They
built it for you.”
    He walked down the Concourse, one shoulder high, in no hurry,
hands in his pockets. Half a block away, he stopped. He didn’t turn
