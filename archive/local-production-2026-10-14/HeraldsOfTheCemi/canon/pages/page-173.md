   Mr. Rodríguez had taken the center of his butcher-paper map down
and replaced it with a floor plan of the Armory, which Vanessa had
sent. He stuck red pins in every exit. “Doors,” he said, tapping them.
“The only thing a building wants to hide is how you leave it.”
   Manny and I would enter on the contributor pass, with the Garden,
the contact mic, the interface, and La Madre in her case, carried in the
open. On the schedule Manny was my percussionist, since Aperture as‐
sumed two familiar faces on one stage counted as a perk. He was also
my heartbeat. Every thirty seconds he would knock twice on the side of
his conga and I would knock twice on my knee. If I missed two an‐
swers in a row, he’d rip the contact cable out of the interface, and I
would be furious and alive.
   “Why thirty seconds?” said Manny.
   “Because it’s longer than I think it is.”
   Mama Tete would walk the procession to the loading doors by half
past nine: bamboo horns, drums, a güira, every Haitian and Dominican
player in the Bronx who could be persuaded to fight over the setlist.
She had asked every one individually, in person, a few of them more
than once. Nobody was being herded into a trap at a free gig.
   “Any of them can walk away,” she told us.
   “We can too,” said Manny.
   “Fine. Hold that thought when you start improvising.”
   Yanaisa would stay outside with her, carrying Yúcahu. Abuela had a
rule about that, delivered over the phone like a commandment: never
both stones in the same hands. If someone took one, the other stayed
free.
   Desmond’s job was to stand in a doorway.
   “That’s it?” said Manny.
   “Twenty-six years on the Bx1,” said Desmond, with dignity. “It’s a
doorway career.”
