                              CHAPTER NINE


                       Santo Domingo
            T R AC K   “Ojalá Que Llueva Café” — Juan Luis Guerra y 440
                       ECHO   the river wall, seventeen years ago




Mamá put the coffee can on the table Sunday night and didn’t say
what was in it.
   It was a Café Bustelo can, the red kind, with a plastic lid gone soft at
the edges, and it had lived behind the rice for as long as I’d been alive.
I’d assumed it held rice.
   “Open it.”
   Cash. Twenties and tens in rubber-banded bundles, soft as cloth
from handling. On top, a folded paper in her slanted handwriting with
a number, $620, and under it a second number crossed out, and a third.
Years of arithmetic.
   “Para cuando yo regresara,” she said. For when I went back. “Thirty-
one years I’ve been going back. It’s a good can.”
   “Má, no.”
   “It’s a round trip. I looked.” Her hands lay flat on the table. “I came
here so you wouldn’t have to go there. I told your Abuela on the phone:
not him. Give me one child who doesn’t have to carry it.” Her voice
didn’t break. It ground. “And now a gentleman puts a basket on Luz’s
mat, and a doctor writes a word, and my mother calls. I’m not stupid,
Juan Carlos. I can count.”
   I thought about a man in a gray coat with a notebook, and the word
count, and said nothing.
