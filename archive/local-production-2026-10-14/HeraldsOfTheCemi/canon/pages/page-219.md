                               THE CHORD



   The hall held still.



The canoe.
   The river in the dark, mangrove roots on either bank, the cold salt
smell coming from the wrong direction. Three canoes in a line. A man
at the bow of the first. An old man with a drum between his knees in
the second. In the third, a woman with a sleeping child against her ribs.
   I’d been told this part. I’d even told it to you, at the beginning, as
though I’d been there. But I had never been where I was now: inside
the third canoe, looking down.
   The child had woken.
   He was not quite two. His fists were shut, and in one of them was
something dark and round, the size of an egg, with a small turned-
away face. He held it the way toddlers hold toast, with complete, unre‐
flecting seriousness. Two canoes ahead, in the arms of the woman
called Yara, a larger stone lay wrapped in soaked cloth against her
chest.
   The current carried them together.
   The boy lifted his fist toward the big stone. He couldn’t reach it. He
leaned out over the black water, held by his mother’s arm, stretching,
and the small stone and the large one pointed at each other across forty
feet of river, and I heard it: a single bright note ringing between them, a
clave struck across the dark.
   “It’s turning with us,” said the boy to the water, in a voice too old for
him.
   He looked down at the stone in his hand.
   “Turn with it.”
   The current had not simply let them through. It turned when the
two stones rang, and the boy felt it turn. They crossed to stay alive,
with the bell still ringing on the bank behind them, and they got across
by moving with what moved. Wherever they landed, the pair would
answer each other, and the earth under them would learn to answer
back.
