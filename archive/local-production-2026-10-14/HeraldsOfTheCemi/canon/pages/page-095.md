    I read the name again. Amaia. I’d never heard it in my life. It sat in
my chest as if it had been waiting there.
    “Esa es la que cruzó,” said my grandmother. She didn’t say more.
She let me read the line a third time. Tres criaturas que escuchan. Three
who listen. A clerk in 1789 copying an older clerk’s note about children
who heard something, and calling them valuable.
    They hadn’t been counting sacks.
    The next page was from 1838, a single sheet from a ledger for a sug‐
ar mill at Haina, columns of names and ages and values in pesos, and,
in a final column, in a different ink, a heading: Rendimiento. Yield. Be‐
side most names, a number. Beside a few, a word. Oye al agua. Hears
the water. No se calla. Doesn’t go quiet. Tamborero.
    And at the bottom of the page, under the totals, in the neat hand of a
man who was pleased:
    Prohibido el tambor desde Pascua. Se toca la campana al empezar el corte.
Rendimiento: más doce por ciento. Se extiende la medida a los demás ingenios.
    Drum prohibited since Easter. The bell rung at the start of the cut‐
ting. Yield up twelve percent. The measure extended to the other mills.
    I put the page down.
    They’d taken the drum out of the field and hung a bell over it. That’s
all it was. They’d taken the noise out of the room, and the people in it
had gone quiet, and the sugar had gone up twelve percent, and some
man in an office on Calle Las Damas had written it down in a pleased
hand and sent the idea to the other mills.
    A classroom with a ceiling baffle and one low tone. A bodega with a
free speaker. A nail salon, a laundromat, a barbershop that had
stopped arguing about the Mets.
    Aperture hadn’t invented a thing. They’d inherited it.
    “Mira la marca,” Abuela said, gently, and tapped the top of the
page.
    It was a printer’s device, engraved, small. A circle of overlapping
leaves like the blades of a camera. Under it, in capitals: ABERTURA Y
CÍA. — FACTORES.
