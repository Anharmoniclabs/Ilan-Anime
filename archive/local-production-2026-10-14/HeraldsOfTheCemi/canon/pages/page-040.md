                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



   I pulled up the loop I’d saved from the raw feed and measured it.
Eight seconds, to the millisecond. The same carrier. The same pulse.
   Nineteen years apart, and the same tone.
   I didn’t sleep. I did the only thing I know to do with something that
scares me, which is build something. By the time the 4 had gone past
thirty times, I had a new visual for Friday’s open mic, and it ran Item 5
backwards. It fed on noise. The louder and messier the room got, the
more color it threw on the wall, and silence turned it gray. I called it
the Garden. If Early Calm was what a room sounds like with the noise
sanded off, the Garden was what it looks like when you let it grow
back.



Friday the café turns into something else.
    Mr. Rodríguez clears the tables to the walls and strings up the lights
he keeps in a box marked NAVIDAD, in March, because the box is the
box. The open mic has survived two rent hikes, a pandemic, and the
summer the landlord turned off the AC to make a point. People come
from blocks away. The chalkboard by the door said, in his handwriting,
TONIGHT: SOMETHING YOU’VE NEVER SAID OUT LOUD (NO
LIMERICKS).
    Sofía had turned the evening into a science project with a Pepsi
budget.
    At the back table she had a clipboard marked INCIDENTS, which
she’d defined as a raised voice, a shove, or a slammed anything, and a
clicker, the kind security guards use to count people at a door. Across
the street, outside the bodega, Doña Fela’s grandson Junior sat on a
milk crate with a second clicker and a Pepsi. Junior was seventeen and
would have counted anything for twenty bucks and a soda. He texted
her the tally every half hour.
    “It’s a control and an experimental site,” she told me, adjusting the
clicker with the focus of a bomb tech. “Same night. Same block. Same
weather. One has the loop and one doesn’t.”
    “It’s a poetry reading.”
