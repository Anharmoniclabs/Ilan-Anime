   Returned.
   By three the hall was filling: Rafa with the palo on his shoulder,
Yenni with the daycare kids in two lines, Desmond with a thermos and
a bucket. Mama Tete stood in the middle of the floor looking up at the
steel roof, and I did not ask what she was thinking.
   At four, Mamá went to the airport.
   She went in her church coat with her hair done, carrying a Café
Bustelo can. I’d offered to drive. She wanted to do this part by herself.
   I went anyway and stood at the back by a pillar.
   They came through the doors at 4:40. Abuela was in her good dress
and the shoes she’d last worn to a funeral, with a plastic bag from the
bodega in one hand and my cousin Yanaisa behind her, a woven sling
across her chest, wearing the expression of a woman who had just been
forbidden to explain a stone to airport security.
   My mother stopped walking.
   Thirty-one years. A can. A phone call that began Altagracia and
ended hiciste bien.
   Abuela set the plastic bag down on the floor of JFK, very carefully,
and opened both arms.
   Two women in church clothes held each other in the middle of ar‐
rivals while a man with a luggage cart said excuse me, excuse me, and,
after a moment, simply went around.
   When they let go, Mamá held out the can.
   “Aquí está la lata.”
   Abuela took it. She tipped it toward the light, looked inside, looked
at my mother.
   “Vacía.”
   “Se la gasté a usted.”
   I spent it on you.
   Abuela laughed, a sound I’d never heard from her, wide open, a
laugh that belonged in a much younger woman’s mouth. “Entonces la
lata cumplió,” she said. Then the can did its job.
