                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



   Manny looked at the pastelito, then at me. I shrugged. He sighed the
way he sighs, a sound like a couch being lifted, which is how he agrees
to everything.
   Mr. Rodríguez dug a conga out of the storage closet. It was an old
one, from the record-shop days, the head loose and slightly tilted.
Manny held it up. “This thing has the personality of my uncle.” He
tapped it, and tipped his head, and tapped again, and with his tongue
stuck out the corner of his mouth he tuned it by ear in about four
seconds, and the room laughed at how easy it looked.
   Then he sat.
   He started the way he always starts, like he was picking up a conver‐
sation he’d left on the other side of the room.
   What I’d tell you is that nobody should be allowed that much talent
and that little regard for himself. Manny drums the way he talks,
loudly and without any plan, and somehow every sentence lands. In
my headphones the room was forty heartbeats, a bar’s worth of conver‐
sation, two babies, a milk steamer. His hands didn’t drown them. They
gave them a floor. One at a time, I felt people stop holding their own
time and borrow his. The two guys arguing about the bullpen stopped
arguing and started nodding in the same place. The steamer’s whistle
slid onto a clean pitch. A baby sighed.
   The Garden went gold.
   Then something came up under the gold that I hadn’t written.
   It rose through the colors like a watermark rising through paper: the
spiral and triangle, nested, twenty feet tall, blue and gold, breathing. I
checked my screen. There was no preset. The Garden was just doing
what I’d built it to do, turning sound into shape, and the sound had a
shape in it, and the shape was the one off my wall.
   The room read it as part of the show. A few phones came up. Some‐
body said, “yo, that’s the AR kid.” Manny kept playing with his eyes
shut, no idea.
   I reached for the kill switch and stopped with my hand an inch
above it.
