                            A NA M E FO R I T



    “What’s the same?”
    “Nobody’s dancing.”
    “What’s it doing to them?”
    Rafa watched Papo sweep the same patch of floor for the third time.
“First it makes you mad. Then it makes you tired. The tired is the part
they want.” He said it the way you give directions to a bus stop. “My
grandmother had a word for what goes out of a man when he’s been
mad too long and has nowhere to put it. El ánimo. The get-up in a per‐
son.” He tipped his chin at the speaker in the corner. “That box takes
it.”
    The third stop was the one I hadn’t wanted.
    The Ballroom was open, because Denise, who runs the arts space,
keeps the door propped on Wednesdays, and a volunteer was hanging
a banner that said SOUND BATH SUNDAYS. I’d been in and out of
that room for six weeks, measuring the brick for the mural. I’d never
looked up.
    In the middle of the ceiling, between two old chandelier hooks, was
a small white speaker, new, with a little decal on it: a camera iris, the
blades overlapping.
    I stood in the doorway and listened.
    It was soft. A kind of pad, warm and slow, the sort of thing a spa
plays when it wants you to lower your shoulders. And under it, in the
same place every eight seconds, the pressed place. In the room where
my mural was going to live. In the room whose ghost I’d watched go
quiet one couple at a time.
    I’d spent all winter thinking the loop came from outside. Some of it
did. Some of it, I understood, had been coming up through the floor.
    “Isn’t it lovely?” Denise had appeared next to me, in a cardigan,
holding a staple gun like a person who’d been handed a staple gun by
life. “It was a gift. From a community partner. Mr. Vale came by to in‐
stall it himself. He sat in the back for an hour with a little notebook,
such a gentleman, he called everyone ‘neighbor.’ The staff all say they
feel calmer. We’ve even noticed fewer fights in the lobby.”
    “Denise. You had a fight in the lobby?”
