                                   RO O F



   There was also a big, quiet man named Desmond, who played the
bucket and drove the Bx1 and who said hello to the entire room and
then ate three plates without comment, and a nineteen-year-old named
Pri, who’d come in with her hood up and sat in the corner with her
phone and a look I recognized. She kept turning her phone face down,
then face up, like she was still deciding whether to show anybody.
   We ate first. That was Mamá’s rule, not a request. Nobody spoke of
the thing until the flan, and then Rafa laid his fork down and said, to
the table, with no preamble, “Tell them what you told me.”
   So I told them. The loop. Early Calm. The ledger. Vale. Founders’
Night.
   When I stopped, nobody said anything. Then Desmond put down
his fork.
   “My bus used to be loud,” he said.
   Everyone turned.
   “Twenty-six years on the Bx1. I know the regulars. The lady who
talks to her bag. The guys who argue about Jeter. Kids doing home‐
work out loud.” He looked at his hands. “In January they got quiet. All
of them. They just ride. I thought it was winter.”
   “The bus has a new announcement tone,” Pri said, from the corner,
not looking up. “It’s softer. They rolled it out in January.”
   I looked at her.
   “We take the Bx1,” said Yenni, slowly. “The daycare got a gift in
January. A sound machine for nap time. The kids don’t fight anymore.
They don’t do anything. They lie there and stare.” She folded her hands
in her lap. “I took it out the day it came. I had a feeling. I put it in the
closet. The director was upset.”
   “You what?” said Sofía.
   “I put it in the closet.”
   “Port-au-Prince,” said Mama Tete.
   Everyone turned again.
   “My cousin Mireille. The market at Croix-des-Bossales went quiet in
January. Free speakers, a company with a leaf.” She folded her napkin
into a square. “A friend in Kingston says the same. They are not only
here.”
