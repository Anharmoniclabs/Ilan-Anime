                         HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



    “I’m a very old man,” said Don Chino, with dignity. “My legs are
tired.”



The supervisor didn’t stop.
    His badge said DUANE. He was big, heavy-shouldered, forty, with a
radio at his lip and the flat eyes of a man who’d finished his training
and been told what happened next. He stepped over Doña Fela’s hat
without breaking stride, and he came down the aisle straight at the
trolley, and what I had was a hunch, a half-beat of lateness, and a stone.
    He caught the strap of the case.
    The heat went through my wrist. La Madre, three feet from my face,
gave a low, wordless complaint, and the whole center platform
flickered in the corner of my eye, the ring of forty, the hum rising, a
leaning in the air toward the thing in his hand.
    I could have narrowed it.
    I knew exactly how. I knew the aim and the pitch and the feeling
that had cracked a window and a boy’s ear. The pipe was a hundred
feet behind me and the steel columns were everywhere, and a man’s
collarbone was ten inches away. Sixty-one percent, I thought. They’d
written it down. They wanted it. A violent participant, in front of elev‐
en hundred witnesses, with four buildings waiting on a signature.
    I let go of the case.
    That’s what I did. I let go. I put both hands up, empty, in front of the
whole hall, and the case swung down toward the floor.
    Duane looked at my hands.
    The pause was enormous. For one second a man trained to restrain a
participant held a stone case in his arms and looked at a participant
who’d done nothing and wanted him to see it.
