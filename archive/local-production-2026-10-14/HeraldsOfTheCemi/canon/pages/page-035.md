                               THE MODEL



    It sat on top of the supply closet, a beige box with a mesh front, right
above the cubbies. After lunch every day, quiet time: lights off, heads
down, a rug shaped like the United States, and out of the speaker came
a tone, low and even, like the waiting room at a dentist’s office. Every‐
one else put their heads on their arms. I’m not exaggerating when I say
everything went flat. The room, the air, my own hands. I couldn’t lie
still. I tapped the leg of my desk, a little rhythm, to push back against it,
because that’s the only thing my body knew to do.
    That was what the teacher wrote down. I know because a woman
with a lanyard came one afternoon and watched me with a clipboard,
and a week later a letter came home.
    My father read it standing in the kitchen with the careful face he
used for the electric bill. He read it twice. He said one word, which was
the number on it, in Spanish, the way you say the number of a bus
you’re not going to take.
    “Nivel dos.”
    My mother took the letter and put it in the drawer where she keeps
the papers that matter: the lease, my father’s death certificate in a
folder she hasn’t opened, the letters from school that nobody ever
answered. She never said another word about it. The drawer closing
was the whole conversation.
    “Level two,” I told Sofía. “It said level two.”
    She scrolled. “There’s a rubric. It’s called the Calm Index. One to
four. It scores…” She read it, and I watched her stop. “‘Atypical re‐
sponse to ambient stimulus.’ Hand-tapping, vocalizing, agitation dur‐
ing rest periods.” She looked up. “That’s you.”
    “That’s me.”
    “That’s a sentence you write when you’re looking for the kids who
react.” She said it flat, deliberately, the way she does when she’s afraid
of getting it wrong. “I’m not saying that’s what it means. I’m saying
that’s what the sentence says. If I were running a test and I needed to
find the kids who didn’t go quiet with everyone else, that’s the line I’d
write.”
