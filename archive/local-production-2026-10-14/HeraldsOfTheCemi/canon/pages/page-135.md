                              PA P E R T R A I L



   “I don’t know yet.” Sofía tapped the underlined line. “They meas‐
ured something at every one of those places. The reports are listed in
the catalog and not attached.”
   In Santo Domingo, in a trunk under my grandmother’s bed, there
was an airmail letter from Rosnaree, County Meath. I’d looked it up on
the plane home. Rosnaree is a mile from Newgrange.



Mama Tete arrived at six with a photograph wrapped in a dish towel.
   She hadn’t said a word to me since the dinner. She set the towel
down on Doña Aurora’s good tablecloth, drew back the corner, and I
saw a studio portrait in sepia: a thin man in a three-piece suit, hands on
his knees, looking into the lens without blinking, jaw set, as if he’d de‐
cided looking away would cost too much. Beside him, on a stand, a tall
drum.
   “Papa Janvier,” said Mama Tete. “My mother’s father. Port-au-Prince
to Harlem in 1923. Came with that drum and eleven dollars.”
   “A drummer,” said Sofía.
   “A houngan’s drummer. For the dances. For the dead.” Tete’s voice
had no tremor in it at all. That was how I knew. “In 1925 a company on
West 125th Street asked him to make records. They paid him forty dol‐
lars for six sides. My mother said he was proud for a week.”
   Sofía had gone very still. “Meridian Sound.”
   “The records were never released.”
   Sofía was already across the room, fumbling through a folder, and
she pulled out a single page: a catalog index, 1926, photographed from
a bound ledger. A column of titles. A narrow right-hand column
headed Estado. Status. And next to a handful of entries, in a neat hand,
a single word.
   Retenido. Withheld.
   “Janvier, six sides,” Sofía read. “Retenido. Twelve Cuban sides. A
West African ensemble. A Dominican palo group from Villa Mella.”
She turned the page toward us. “They didn’t just record them. They
bought them, and then they put them in a drawer.”
