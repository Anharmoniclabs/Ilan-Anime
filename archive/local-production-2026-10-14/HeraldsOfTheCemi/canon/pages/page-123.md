                      CHAPTER THIRTEEN


                                 Roof
                      T R AC K   “Hot Hot Hot” — Arrow
                  ECHO   Manny’s roof, the blackout, July 1977




Manny’s roof is where we’ve told each other everything since we
were fifteen.
   It’s a tar beach: gravel and black rubber, a rusted water tower, a TV
antenna that hasn’t received a signal since the Clinton administration,
and a view that goes all the way down the Concourse to the lights over
Yankee Stadium. We came up here the night his father left. We came up
here the night mine died. We’ve come up here a thousand other nights
for no reason at all, just to sit with our backs against the chimney and
eat something fried. I’d never lied to him on this roof. That was exactly
the problem.
   He’d brought a towel and a bag of tostones, and a six-pack he’d nev‐
er opened, because Manny buys beer to feel like a man in a movie and
then doesn’t drink it.
   “Okay,” he said, sitting down. “Hit me.”
   So I told him.
   I did it plainly, because Manny can’t take a metaphor; he takes
whatever you say and puts it on the table. I told him about the coin and
the stone and the ledger. I told him my family had kept a thing for five
hundred years, and a company with an iris for a logo had been trying
to take it that long, and that what I could do, the hearing, the holding,
was old, and that he could do it too.
