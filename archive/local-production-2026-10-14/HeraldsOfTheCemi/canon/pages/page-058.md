                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



    He stood by the gate in a good gray jacket and a ball cap pulled low,
with a stack of glossy paper and a smile that had been professionally
trained to be warm. The flyer said A CALMER BLOCK STARTS HERE,
and under it: Aperture Civic · Community Listening Sessions begin in April
· Free food · Free sound bath. I took one, which I’m embarrassed about.
You can’t not take a flyer from a man who’s smiling at you. That’s how
they get you.
    It was the walk that stopped me.
    Left shoulder dipping on the step, like he was avoiding a puddle
only he could see. I’d watched that walk go down the stairs of the
fourth floor, every day, for twelve years. Then I saw the eyebrow. A pale
line through the left one, thin as a pencil mark, from a fall off our fire
escape when we were nine.
    “Mikey?”
    The guy turned. His face did nothing. It was a very calm nothing,
like a screen between channels.
    “Have a good Sunday, brother,” he said, and kept smiling, and gave
a flyer to a woman with a stroller.
    I stood there with my mouth open. Mikey from four. Mikey, who’d
gotten the three, who’d stopped answering my texts two years ago,
whose mother had stopped answering her door. Mikey would never
have said brother in his life. He said bro, like a normal person, with his
whole chin.
    I told myself it was a coincidence, number forty-eight, and I walked
into the clearing, and I carried the flyer with me like it was warm.



I’d expected a circle of drummers and a lot of incense. I got about fif‐
teen people in a loose ring, drums of every kind, and a woman selling
cut mango and chicharrón out of a cooler. There were congas and a
couple of cajones and a tall palo drum between the knees of a lean old
man, and a very large man on a five-gallon bucket who I would learn
within minutes was one of the best players there. Kids ran at the edges.
