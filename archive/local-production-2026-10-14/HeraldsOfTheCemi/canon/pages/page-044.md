                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ



   I was clapping with everyone else, shaky, ravenous, my hands not
fully mine. A clock over the register said the set had lasted twenty-six
minutes. I remembered about six.
   And then I looked past the applause, past the lights and the phones,
to the door, and a woman there wasn’t looking at the wall.
   She was old, but not frail. Old the way a building is old, like she’d
been on the block longer than the things around her and had opinions
about all of them. Silver hair pulled back hard. A cardigan the color of
dried tobacco. A canvas grocery bag in one hand, like she’d been walk‐
ing home and heard a drum and stopped to listen.
   She wasn’t looking at the glyph. She was looking at me.
   She didn’t flinch at the glyph or the noise. She watched me with her
head tipped a little, unhurried, like she’d been told what time I’d show
up and I was right on it.
   She nodded once, small, like a person checking a name off a list.
There you are.
   Then she turned and went out into the street, and the bell over the
door rang behind her.
   I went after her. I got through four people and a stroller, and by the
time I hit the sidewalk the street was empty both ways. No cardigan, no
bag. Just the El overhead and the block under it, and across the street,
on the third floor of the building in the blue netting, one window lit.
   The man was there. He wasn’t facing us this time. He was at a desk,
with his head bowed, and his arm was moving, a small neat motion,
the way a man writes when he’s counting. As I watched, he looked up,
straight at me, across the whole width of Jerome Avenue, and reached
out and drew the blind.



“Two,” Sofía said.
   She was sitting on the curb with her clipboard in her lap and Juni‐
or’s last text still lit on her phone. She looked like she’d been sat down
by a hand.
