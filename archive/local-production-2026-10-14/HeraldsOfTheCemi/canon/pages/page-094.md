    “¿Qué dice, Mami?” the girl asked.
    “No sé.” The woman folded it in four. “Pesa.”
    It weighs.
    “Es de las que tienen nombre,” she said. “Una página con nombre
pesa más.” One with a name weighs more. She laid it in a flour tin and
closed the lid. “Guárdala. Una al año, hasta que alguien sepa leer.”
    One a year, until somebody can read.
    The girl nodded and pressed her hands flat against her knees.
    “Veintiocho páginas,” said Abuela behind me, in the doorway, in the
present. “My mother cleaned their offices on Calle Las Damas for
twenty-eight years. They put the pages they were going to burn in a bin
by the door. She took one every year, under her blouse.” She came in
and lowered herself onto the bed with a small sound. “I learned to read
at the night school. For this.”



She laid them out on the bedspread, the old pages in their plastic, in or‐
der. The first thing I noticed was how many different hands there were.
The second was that every page, at the top, had a mark.
   The oldest was a fair copy dated 1789 and headed LIBRO DE FU‐
GAS. Book of Escapes. A clerk had copied it, in brown ink, out of a still
older book that had fallen apart. Most of the page was dates and num‐
bers. One entry in the middle was longer than the others, because
somebody had cared.
   I read it aloud. My Spanish isn’t good enough for old Spanish, and
Abuela fed me the words I stumbled on.
   1523. La mujer Amaia, de veintiséis años, con un hijo de veintidós meses, y
tres criaturas que escuchan, huidos por el río en tres canoas, con un anciano
tamborero y otros. Valiosos. Se recomienda recobrarlos. No se recobraron.
   The woman Amaia, twenty-six, with a son of twenty-two months,
