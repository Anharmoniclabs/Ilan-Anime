                             A NA M E FO R I T



    “You put ‘tapping’ as your reason for visit.”
    “It seemed right.”
    “In fourteen years, nobody’s ever written ‘tapping.’ They write ‘fo‐
cus’ or ‘my wife made me.’” The corner of her mouth moved. “I like it.”
    She asked me to tell her about being six. I told her about a tone and
a clipboard and a letter in a drawer. I said a number. She stopped typ‐
ing.
    “The Calm Index?”
    “You know it?”
    “I know the phrase.” She set the tablet face-down on her knee. “I
won’t go further than I can prove, and I can’t prove much. But I’ve had
four patients in the last two years, all adults, all from the same three
elementary schools in this zip code. All flagged at six or seven. All with
the same line in the old records.” She looked at me. “I wrote it down. I
didn’t know what it meant.”
    “Somebody does.”
    “Then somebody can put it on paper.” Dry. “I’ll need it in writing
before I say another word.”
    Then she did the thing I’d been waiting for, with the neutral kind‐
ness of someone who’d done it before.
    “I want to be careful. A real evaluation takes a battery of tests and a
second visit, and we’ll do all of that. But I’ll tell you what I’m fairly
confident I’m looking at, because I think you’ve been waiting a long
time for someone to just say it.”
    And she said it. Two words, then a third. ADHD. Combined presenta‐
tion. It came with a code that an insurance company understands.
    I’d braced for a door swinging open, or a blow. What I got was a
click, like a key in a lock it was cut for.
    “Does it fix anything?” I said. “The name?”
    “No.” She didn’t soften it. “It’s not a cure. We’ll talk about tools, ac‐
commodations, and medication if you want it. But the name doesn’t
change a single fact about you. Everything you did yesterday, you’ll do
tomorrow. What it changes is what you’ve been calling it.” She picked
up her pen. “You’ve been calling it something that sounds like a char‐
acter flaw. It isn’t one.”
