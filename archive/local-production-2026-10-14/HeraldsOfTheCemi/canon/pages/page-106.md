                        HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ




The colmado’s owner was a stout woman in a visor, wiping a counter. I
asked her, in my worst Spanish, whether she could turn off the speaker
for five minutes.
   “Es de una compañía,” she said. “I can’t. It’s on an app.” She
shrugged and looked at the ceiling. “Honestly? It’s on, it’s off, I don’t
see.”
   “No me digas,” I said.
   “Yo no vi nada,” said the woman, and went back to her counter and
wiped it very thoroughly, looking in the other direction.
   So I pulled the plug.
   It was just a cord. I should be embarrassed how long I’d spent think‐
ing this was supernatural. It was a white box on a bracket with a power
cable running into the wall, and I unplugged it, and the bachata
stopped, and the batey went quiet for one clear second.
   Then Eliseo, who’d been waiting with his head in his hands, said
into the quiet, hoarsely, “Otra vez. Desde el principio.” Again. From the
top.
   The drums started. The horns came in.
   On the beat.
   I don’t know how to describe the next minute without sounding like
I’m making it up. Twenty-two people stopped hearing each other as
late. The trumpets turned over, one note at a time, and the drums
leaned in to catch them, and the old Haitian woman’s face did some‐
thing I won’t forget, and the young drummer who’d said ustedes llegan
tarde looked across the yard at her with his mouth open, as if he had
just discovered she could sing. The whole thing locked. A tune ap‐
peared that nobody was holding and everybody was.
   The old woman lowered her trumpet when it ended. She said, to
nobody, “Así era.”
   That’s how it used to be.
   My nose was bleeding a little. I wiped it on my wrist. Abuela
watched me from the shade with her hands folded and smiled all the
way up to her eyes.
