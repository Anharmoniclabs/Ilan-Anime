                                 MIKEY



   “Tú eres el hijo de Altagracia,” she said. “El que tocaba la pared.”
   The one who knocked on the wall. I hadn’t known she knew about
that.
   “Doña Luz. I’m sorry I didn’t come before.”
   “Sorry.” She tasted the word. “He had a lot of names for you. Juan‐
qui. Mi hermano. The one with the headphones.” A pause, in which a
woman in the TV slapped a man. “He wasn’t mad at you, you under‐
stand. That was the worst part. He just stopped saying your name.”



She told it in order, with no adjectives, a story she had told herself
every night for years.
   “In first grade a woman came with a clipboard. A letter came. The
letter said tres. Three, out of four. The other mothers looked at me in
the hall like I’d been issued something.” She spread her hands on the
table. “A month later a man came to the apartment. A gentleman. Gray
coat. He sat right where you’re sitting, mijo. He asked me, ‘May I?’ be‐
fore he sat. Nobody asks me that.”
   I had known that was coming. It still went through me like cold wa‐
ter.
   “He had a little notebook. He wrote while I talked. He called me
neighbor. He said my Mikey was exceptionally sensitive, and that there
were programs. Free. Wellness, he called it. A tablet when he turned
twelve. A job at nineteen, a ‘community ambassador.’ A stipend.” She
laughed, once, with no air in it. “I had two jobs. I said yes.”
   “Did you—”
   “And it worked. God forgive me, mijo, it worked. He was a loud boy.
He broke things. He talked to walls, to the radiator, to the stairs. I was
so tired.” Her hands didn’t move on the table. “And one day he was
calm. Se calmó. He sat at that table and ate what I put in front of him
and said thank you. And I’d have given my right hand for one broken
plate.”
   The telenovela cut to a commercial. A woman in a spa robe told us
to find our balance.
