                         HERALDS OF THE CEMÍ




For a long time I just listened. Rafa was right. I wasn’t ready to play,
and I didn’t want to be, because I didn’t have to run the board, or watch
the meters, or perform sound for someone else. I sat on the stone ring
with my palms flat on it, the concrete cold and faintly humming, and I
let the circle go through me.
    Here’s what I heard.
    Fifteen separate clocks, each running its own time. The big guy on
the bucket had a pulse a hair behind everything, as if he’d never once
been late in his life and would be damned if he’d ever be early. The
güira ran tight and fast, a nervous cricket. A kid, maybe eleven, on a
cowbell, was playing a pattern that didn’t seem to agree with anyone
and somehow held the whole thing up. Rafa’s drum didn’t keep time at
all; it commented on it. The circle wasn’t the sum of those parts. It was
the places where they didn’t line up, the little gaps and collisions, and the
way each player listened for the gap and filled it, so the spaces became
part of the music.
    Most people can hold one line. A teacher hears one line. My whole
life, I’d been told my attention was the defect, that it went everywhere
at once and never stayed anywhere, forty tabs open and three of them
playing music. And here, for the first time, was a thing that needed
forty tabs. A thing that only made sense if you held all of it at once. I
could hear every player, and the spaces between them, and the shape
the spaces made in the air, and it wasn’t overwhelming. It was the most
at home my head had ever been, and I was about to cry about it in pub‐
lic, which I decided to do later.
    Rafa was watching me. He’d been watching the whole time.
    “Close your eyes,” he said, not unkindly, in the middle of a phrase,
like it cost him nothing. “Tell me what Desmond’s doing.”
    “Who’s Desmond?”
    “The bucket.”
    “He’s…” I listened. “He’s playing the end of the bar and the start of
the next one, like he’s not sure where one stops. He’s late on purpose.
