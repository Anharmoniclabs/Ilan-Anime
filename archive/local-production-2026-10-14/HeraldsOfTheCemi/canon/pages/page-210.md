   Neither side marched ahead. When the old woman with the long

   Mama Tete walked beside Yanaisa, who carried Yúcahu in her sling
against her chest, with the blue skirt she’d hemmed swinging at her
ankles. She didn’t lead the music. She watched the room. That’s what
an elder does.
   I leaned on the loading wall to keep myself standing, and braced a
forearm on the brick.
   The brick was cold. It was very old. The Armory.
   It was 1913, and there was scaffolding. A mason on a plank, forty
feet up, in a flat cap, working a trowel along the course of the wall. He
wasn’t looking at his work. He was listening to it. He tapped each brick
with the handle of the trowel, lightly, one after another, and put his
head to one side. Tink. Tink. Tink. A good brick answered in a bright
voice. One brick, a little farther along, gave a flat, dull answer, and he
stopped, and pried it out with the point of his trowel, and set a new
one in its place.
   He did it a thousand times that day. He hummed while he did it.
   Somebody had put an ear to this wall before it was finished, and
had decided what it was allowed to hold.
   The mason went on. The scaffolding faded. The procession came
through the door.
